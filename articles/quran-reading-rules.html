Quran Stopping Signs: 18 Terms and How to Read Them Carefully

Talqeen Academy · Updated 4 October 2026, 12:56:34 (UTC+03:00)

Careful Quran recitation includes deciding where to stop and where to begin again. The Quran is Allah's revealed word, and respecting its wording and meaning requires more than recognising individual letters.

Printed stopping marks help guide recitation, but notation differs between Mushaf editions. The eighteen items below preserve the source's topics while correcting misleading labels and distinguishing established guidance from unverified claims. Learn the legend in your own edition with a qualified teacher.

Before Learning the Symbols

Waqf is a stop in recitation, normally allowing a breath with the intention of continuing. Sakt is a brief interruption without taking a breath. A complete, sufficient or good stop describes a relationship of meaning; a decorative ayah marker alone does not classify every ending as a complete stop.

The source promised beautiful recitation without mistakes from knowing these symbols. Signs support learning, but pronunciation, Tajweed, meaning and suitable restarting points also need practice.

1. The Ayah-End Marker and Waqf Tam

A circular or ornamental numbered marker identifies the end of an ayah. It is not necessarily at the end of a printed line, and not every ayah ending is technically waqf tam, a complete stop in meaning.

The source treated every such marker as a compulsory perfect stop whose omission changes the meaning. That is too broad. Pausing at ayah endings is an important recitation practice, while connecting a passage and judging its meaning requires informed guidance.

2. م — Waqf Lazim

A stopping meem indicates a required stop in the edition's notation, used where connection could suggest an unintended meaning. Learn its exact location and how to resume. Do not confuse a stopping sign above the text with a meem that is part of a word.

3. ط — Waqf Mutlaq

Some editions use ta for a strong or absolute stopping indication. A normal stop permits taking a breath before resuming. The sign concerns suitable division of the recitation, rather than merely creating a pleasant rhythm.

Structured stopping can make longer passages manageable. Its meaning must still be understood through the convention of the printed Mushaf.

4. ج — Waqf Jaiz

Jeem commonly marks a permissible stop where stopping or joining are both allowed. The original text omitted the symbol from this heading and suggested a general preference for stopping. That preference should not be assumed for every jaiz sign.

A pause may give time to consider the passage, but personal reflection is different from the notation's technical meaning. Follow the indicated reading options.

5. ز — Continuation Preferred in Some Editions

The source called this waqf mujawwaz: joining is preferred, while stopping remains possible under the relevant convention. It is not a command to continue beyond comfortable breath.

If a necessary stop interrupts a connected meaning, ask a teacher where to restart so the next phrase is not misleading on its own.

6. ص — A Licensed Stop

Some notation systems use sad for a permitted stop when a reader needs a break, while continuation is preferred. The source connected it with fatigue or exhaustion.

A physical need to breathe should be addressed with an appropriate stop and restart. The sign should not be turned into a test of whether the learner is sufficiently tired to deserve a breath.

7. صلى — Joining Is Preferable

This mark allows stopping while indicating a preference for joining. The source's example is in Al-Anam 6:17: وَإِنْ يَمْسَسْكَ اللَّهُ بِضُرٍّ فَلا كَاشِفَ لَهُ إِلا هُوَ ۖ وَإِنْ يَمْسَسْكَ بِخَيْرٍ فَهُوَ عَلَى كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ. The indicated location follows هُوَ, not simply the earlier word لَهُ.

Learn the exact placement rather than moving the sign to whichever nearby word seems convenient.

8. قلى — Stopping Is Preferable

The source heading incorrectly said “better not to stop,” although its explanation meant the opposite. Qala indicates that stopping is preferable while joining remains permitted.

Its example concerns Al-Kahf 18:22: قُلْ رَبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِمْ مَا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلا قَلِيلٌ ۗ فَلا تُمَارِ فِيهِمْ. The shown sign follows قَلِيلٌ. This excerpt illustrates the location, not the whole ayah.

9. س — Saktah

A saktah mark calls for a short interruption of sound without taking a new breath, as taught for the relevant transmitted reading. It differs from an ordinary breathing stop.

Learn these locations through a qualified reciter. Their application should not be generalised indiscriminately across every riwayah.

10. لا — Avoid Stopping Here

This sign commonly warns against stopping at a location where the following words are closely connected in meaning. “No need to stop,” the source's heading, understated that warning.

The source example is from An-Nahl 16:32: الَّذِينَ تَتَوَفَّاهُمُ الْمَلائِكَةُ طَيِّبِينَ ۙ يَقُولُونَ سَلامٌ عَلَيْكُمُ ادْخُلُوا الْجَنَّةَ. In the shown notation, the warning follows طَيِّبِينَ. The unrelated website instruction copied into the original quotation was an extraction artifact, not Quranic text.

If breath runs out, stop safely and resume from a suitable preceding point taught by the instructor. The handling of an ayah-end notation may also require separate explanation.

11. Paired Three-Dot Marks — Waqf Muanqah

The paired marks identify alternatives: if you stop at one indicated position, do not also stop at the other within that connected reading. They are often represented with three dots arranged in a triangle.

The source used Al-Baqarah 2:2: ذَلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لا رَيْبَ ۛ فِيهِ ۛ هُدًى لِلْمُتَّقِينَ. Here the indicated alternatives are after رَيْبَ and فِيهِ. A necessary interruption and a deliberate chosen stop need suitable restarting guidance.

12. صل — An Edition-Dependent Continuation Label

The source called this qad yusal and described a permissible pause with a preference for continuation. Similar abbreviations can have edition-specific meanings. Do not assume that صل is a separate universal sign distinct from every form of صلى.

Check the legend instead of treating the phrase as a mandatory stop. Where the convention allows a pause, its preference still needs to be understood.

13. قف — “Stop”

Qif is an instruction meaning “stop.” Some explanatory traditions print it at selected locations. The source described it as an anticipation mark analogous to an English full stop; that comparison cannot determine Quranic stopping rules.

Its practical application comes from the edition and informed recitation teaching, including a suitable restart.

14. وقفة — Waqfah

The source presented waqfah as a longer breathless pause than saktah. That definition should not be imposed universally on every edition or reading tradition.

Where an edition uses this label, ask what duration and breathing convention it intends. Do not turn an unclear label into an instruction to hold the breath for an arbitrary long period.

15. ك — “Likewise” in Some Notation

The source connected kaf with kadhalik, meaning “likewise,” and said it repeats an earlier sign's instruction. Its additional claim that it always carries the same meaning as لا was inconsistent with that description.

Where this convention exists, identify the earlier instruction it refers to. Kaf is not automatically a universal prohibition on stopping.

16. Waqf Ghufran — A Claimed Forgiveness Stop

The source described a label meaning a forgiveness stop and instructed readers to recite a short prayer there. That assertion was not accompanied by a reliable reference establishing a universal Quran-reading rule.

Seeking Allah's forgiveness is meaningful worship. A printed label, however, does not by itself prove that a particular additional supplication is prescribed at that location. Establish the basis with a qualified teacher before treating it as required practice.

17. Waqf Manzil — A Claimed Stop of Jibril

The source attributed this stop to places where Jibril paused while conveying revelation. Such a historical attribution needs a reliable transmitted basis, not merely a name printed in a local guide.

Preserve the distinction between a juz or manzil division used for reading schedules and a proved prophetic stopping practice. The original claim should not be presented as established evidence without verification.

18. Waqf al-Nabi — Prophetic Recitation Practice

Learning how the Prophet recited is part of studying the transmitted recitation tradition. The source referred to particular prophetic pauses without identifying a specific symbol or report for its locations.

Do not label arbitrary stops as Sunnah. A qualified teacher can explain what a reliable narration or a transmitted reading supports and how that relates to the printed notation.
