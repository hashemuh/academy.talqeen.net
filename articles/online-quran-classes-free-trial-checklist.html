Free Trial Lesson Checklist: What to Look For in an Online Quran Class

Talqeen Academy · Updated 3 October 2026

A free trial is the best information you will get before committing. Used well, it tells you in thirty minutes what no website can: whether the teacher listens, whether the student is comfortable, and whether the plan makes sense.

Use the checklist below in three stages. You can print it or copy it into your phone notes.

In this article

  1. Before the trial
  2. During the trial: what to observe
  3. Questions to ask the teacher
  4. After the trial: a simple scorecard
  5. What to do next
  6. Frequently asked questions

Before the trial

  • Decide the goal in one sentence: "read fluently", "learn Tajweed", "start Hifz", "understand Arabic".
  • Note the student's level honestly, even roughly. "Knows letters but joins slowly" is enough.
  • Choose a time when the student is alert, not straight after school if they are exhausted.
  • Test your camera, microphone and internet five minutes early. Have the mushaf ready.

If you are booking with Talqeen Academy, the trial page explains what to send on WhatsApp: the student's name and age, the course, current level, and preferred days and times with your time zone. The Academy aims to arrange the trial within 24 hours.

During the trial: what to observe

What to watchA good signA warning sign
Level checkTeacher listens to the student read before choosing a planStarts teaching a fixed script without hearing the student
Talking timeStudent recites for much of the lessonTeacher does most of the speaking
CorrectionMistakes are named, explained and repeated until fixedMistakes ignored or corrected harshly
ToneWarm, patient, specific encouragementRushed or discouraging
PlanA clear next step and small homeworkVague promises about "fast results"
ComfortStudent relaxes as the lesson goes onStudent stays anxious or silent

Questions to ask the teacher

  • What would the first month look like for this student?
  • How will you tell me whether the student is improving?
  • What should we practise between lessons, and for how long?
  • What happens if the student misses a lesson or we need to change the time?
  • Which method do you use for beginners? (For reading, Talqeen uses the Noor Al-Bayan approach of teaching all 28 letters with fatha first before adding other vowels.)

After the trial: a simple scorecard

Within an hour of the lesson, while it is fresh, give each item a score from 1 to 5:

  1. Did the teacher listen before teaching?
  2. Were the corrections clear?
  3. Was the student comfortable?
  4. Did we leave with a concrete plan?
  5. Would the student want to come back?

Total of 20 or more is a strong sign. Between 15 and 19, consider a second trial or a different teacher. Below 15, trust your instinct.

What to do next

If the fit is good, agree the schedule that you can keep, not the ideal one. Two or three regular lessons a week beats five that get cancelled. Check the current prices so there are no surprises, and decide the class length with the teacher: 30, 45 or 60 minutes.

If it is not a fit, say so politely and ask whether another teacher is available. Matching the right teacher to the student is part of what a good academy does.
