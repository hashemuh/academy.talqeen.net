How to Read the Quran in Arabic: A Beginner’s Guide

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

The Quran is in Arabic, while translations offer interpretations of its meanings in other languages. A beginner who wants to read the original text needs to connect written letters and signs with their correct sounds. Listening, practice and appropriate teaching make that process more manageable.

The source includes a saying that the Quran reciter and listener have an equal share of reward. It provides no authenticated reference for that wording, so it should not be presented as a verified prophetic quotation. Listening is still a useful part of learning: use it alongside reading and feedback rather than as a substitute for practising yourself.

7 Steps to Read the Quran in Arabic

1. Learn the alphabet and reading signs

Arabic has twenty-eight letters in its usual alphabet listing, beginning with alif and ending with ya. Recognise their individual and joined forms, and learn to distinguish similar shapes by their dots and position. A teacher’s model helps you connect each shape with its articulation.

The source groups eight marks under “vowels”: fathah, kasrah, dammah, sukoon, shaddah, fathatan, kasratan and dammatan. More precisely, fathah, kasrah and dammah represent short vowels; the three tanween signs add the relevant vowel with an n sound in suitable contexts. Sukoon marks the absence of a vowel, and shaddah marks doubling. They are not all vowels.

These transliterations are introductory prompts, not a replacement for Arabic audio. Fathah on ba produces a short “ba”; adding an appropriate alif after it can represent a longer “baa.” The source’s example describing alif plus ba as simply “ba” needs this distinction to avoid confusing short and long vowels.

2. Work through a structured foundation such as Nurul Bayan

The source names Nurul Bayan, attributed to Tariq Al-Saeed, as a foundation for learning to read. It describes an approximately forty-five-page book covering letters, joined forms, words, sentences and Tajweed. Page counts and arrangements can differ by edition, so check the actual text your teacher uses.

A suitable foundation should guide you from easier patterns to more complex ones and include enough independent practice to show understanding. Qaida is another common starting route. Choose a coherent programme rather than repeatedly switching books before mastering their early lessons.

3. Apply the foundations to a short passage

Practise the patterns you have learned in a manageable surah or ayah. Short passages can reduce the load while you are still recognising letters and signs. Read with a teacher or a suitable recording, then try the same portion independently.

The source contrasts this with beginning immediately on longer surahs such as Al-Baqarah, Al-Imran and An-Nisa. That is a practical sequencing suggestion, not a prohibition on reading them. A teacher can also choose a brief passage from a longer surah when it fits the lesson.

A one- or two-minute passage is an example of a small practice unit, not a required duration or a target reading speed. Stop where the text and teacher’s guidance allow, and keep accuracy central.

4. Repeat to develop smoother reading

Read a familiar short passage, such as An-Nas, several times with attention to recurring errors. Repetition can reduce hesitation as the printed patterns become familiar. Check that you are still following the words rather than recalling the sound without looking.

The source suggests timing practice to notice increased speed. A timer is optional and should never encourage rushing. Compare clarity and independence first; a faster reading that drops letters or shortens required sounds is not meaningful progress.

5. Add small, accurate memorisation targets

Memorising a short, correctly read passage can reinforce familiarity. Listen to a reliable model, repeat a suitable portion and review it on later days. Reading and memorisation can support each other when neither is used to conceal weak pronunciation.

The source states that scholars have prioritised Hifz before fields such as Fiqh, Hadith and Usul. This describes a claimed learning sequence, not a universal requirement to postpone all essential religious learning until the Quran is memorised. Ask a qualified teacher for a balanced programme appropriate to your circumstances.

6. Learn meanings without guessing recitation rules

A translation and suitable Tafsir can help you understand what you read. Meaning can also draw attention to the relationship between phrases, but correct stopping and restarting require recitation guidance. Do not choose pauses solely from an English translation or personal guess.

Study a manageable portion of meaning alongside the Arabic, and ask when an explanation is unclear. Continue with understanding the Quran word by word and a structured approach to Quran study.

7. Learn directly from a teacher with relevant knowledge

A teacher who understands Arabic reading and Quran recitation can listen to your performance, explain an error and model the correction. Native-language status alone does not establish teaching ability or the required recitation knowledge. Assess actual instruction and feedback.

The source attributes a statement to Sheikh Muhammad Shummuni contrasting direct learning from a scholar with reliance on books, and says that book-only study is not sufficient to establish someone as a holder of knowledge. No original citation is supplied, so preserve this as a reported attribution rather than an authenticated quotation.

It also attributes to “Al-Imam Al-Gazari” a claim about scholarly agreement on receiving Quran recitation through teachers and its transmission back to the Prophet. The original reference and the scholar’s exact identification are not supplied. The useful learning point is the importance of accurate oral instruction; this guide does not verify that wording or a universal consensus.

Turn the Steps into a Repeatable Practice Plan

Review familiar letters and signs, read a small new example and return to a passage already checked by the teacher. Keep a note of the particular sound or connection that needs attention. Read it accurately before trying to increase the amount.

Include listening and meaning, while keeping their roles clear: listening provides a model, meaning supports understanding and active reading builds the skill you are learning. Consistency and feedback help these activities work together.

Choose a Clear Route into Arabic Quran Reading

A structured foundation, short passages, careful repetition and direct feedback give beginners a practical path forward. The source concludes by promoting a Quranic Arabic programme. Compare the actual learning goals and teaching arrangements before enrolling, whether the programme focuses on reading, language understanding or both.
