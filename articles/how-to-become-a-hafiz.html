How to Become a Hafiz: 8 Steps for a Sustainable Hifz Journey

Talqeen Academy · Updated 10 October 20265 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00) · Hifz

Becoming a Hafiz means memorising the entire Quran and continuing to maintain that memorisation. The journey needs a clear plan, accurate recitation and regular review. A large new-learning target is not useful if earlier passages become unreliable.

The source recommends teacher guidance, a consistent routine, short starting passages, repetition and listening. These eight steps can help you organise the work, while keeping the pace suited to your circumstances. See the difference between a Qari and a Hafiz to understand the connected learning goals.

8 Steps Towards Becoming a Hafiz

1. Choose a teacher with relevant knowledge

A suitable Qari or Hafiz can listen to your reading, correct pronunciation and help plan memorisation. The title alone does not establish teaching ability: observe how the teacher explains a mistake and adapts the next task.

  • Ask for guidance matched to your starting level.
  • Correct reading errors before they become memorised habits.
  • Agree on a method for learning, testing and reviewing.
  • Make sure accountability remains supportive rather than pressuring.

A teacher can help you stay consistent without guaranteeing a fixed completion time. Use the Quran teacher selection guide to prepare questions.

2. Establish a realistic daily schedule

The source offers five- or six-day new-learning plans, with a possible break from new memorisation on Friday or another suitable day. These are examples. Choose recurring periods that fit your energy, work, school and family responsibilities.

  • Begin with an achievable amount of new material.
  • Allow sufficient time to read it accurately.
  • Include review in the daily plan.
  • Check the plan after difficult weeks and adjust it where necessary.

A flexible timetable still needs structure. Record what was learned and what needs another check so a missed session does not disappear from the plan.

3. Start with manageable surahs

The source proposes Al-Fatihah, Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas, followed later by longer targets such as Yaseen or Al-Mulk. This is one practical route, not a required order. A teacher can choose material according to your actual reading ability.

  • Al-Fatihah: Surah 1, ayahs 1–7 in the commonly used numbering.
  • Al-Ikhlas: Surah 112, ayahs 1–4.
  • Al-Falaq: Surah 113, ayahs 1–5.
  • An-Nas: Surah 114, ayahs 1–6.

Short surahs can provide early milestones, but still require accurate pronunciation. Keep them in review after moving forward. Continue with a structured surah-learning sequence.

4. Keep Muraja’ah alongside new learning

Muraja’ah, or revision, protects what you have already learned. Review recent material and older passages, rather than checking only the most recently memorised ayah. As the total grows, the review routine needs to grow with it.

The source cites Sahih al-Bukhari 5033: “Keep refreshing your knowledge of the Qur’an, for I swear by Him in Whose Hand my life is, it is more prone to escaping than camels that are hobbled.” The comparison emphasises continued attention to memorisation.

Choose realistic review allocations with the teacher. Explore Talqeen Academy’s course options if you need help organising the new-learning and revision workload.

5. Divide long ayahs into suitable portions

A long ayah can be practised in smaller parts before being joined. Use suitable boundaries and have the teacher explain where to pause and how to restart. Do not confuse a learning portion with a separate ayah.

The source uses the opening of Ayat al-Kursi, Al-Baqarah 2:255: اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ. Its meaning begins by affirming Allah, the only deity worthy of worship, the Ever-Living and Sustainer.

Read that portion accurately, learn the next appropriate part and then practise the connections. Return to the full ayah so segmented practice produces connected recall.

6. Use repetition with correction and recall checks

The source suggests ten to fifteen repetitions of an ayah, repeating after Fajr and writing verses as additional exercises. Keep each suggestion in its proper role.

  • Repetition: use a clear model and correct errors before continuing; a fixed count does not guarantee retention.
  • Timing: after Fajr may suit your routine, but is not a universal memory requirement.
  • Writing: copying can support attention to the words where useful; check the text carefully and handle it appropriately.

Move from reading to independent recall, then review later. A familiar-sounding immediate repetition is not enough to establish lasting memorisation.

7. Listen to a suitable recitation

Recordings can provide a model for pronunciation and connected reading. The source names five reciters: Sheikh Abdul Rahman Al-Sudais, Sheikh Mishary Rashid Alafasy, Sheikh Saad Al-Ghamdi, Sheikh Maher Al-Muaiqly and Sheikh Al-Minshawi.

Select an actual recording that fits the task and the teacher’s recitation. Follow the text, listen to a manageable portion and repeat. The pace varies by recording, and audio cannot assess your own performance.

Combine listening with active reading and feedback. This supports accuracy without promising that every learner will memorise faster through the same method.

8. Reinforce secure passages in Salah

Reciting accurately learned portions during daily prayer can make review meaningful and part of ordinary worship. Follow appropriate prayer practice and use material that has been checked.

The source describes this as revision without extra study time and as a way to avoid forgetting. Prayer can reinforce learning, but does not replace separate sessions for stopping, correction and assessment, nor does it guarantee that nothing will be forgotten.

How Long Does Becoming a Hafiz Take?

The source gives three to five years as a general example, around two years for some intensive learners, and five to eight years or longer for busy adults. It supplies no study establishing these as typical population averages.

Actual time depends on the starting level, daily workload, interruptions, passage difficulty and revision. Discuss both new-learning progress and the security of earlier material. Compare a two-year plan with an intensive six-month example without treating either as a universal deadline.

Keep Accuracy and Retention at the Centre

A sound Hifz plan combines suitable instruction, manageable portions, repeated checking and ongoing Muraja’ah. Self-practice is important, while a capable listener helps you identify mistakes and keep the learning reliable.

The source advertises programmes for children and adults with qualified teachers. Evaluate the actual lesson and support rather than a claim that a platform is the “best.” Continue with the Quran memorisation guide to organise the continuing journey.
