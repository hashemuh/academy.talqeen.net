Online Quran Classes: A Complete Guide for Families

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 00:26:20 (UTC+03:00)

If you are weighing up online Quran classes for your child, for yourself, or for the whole household, the options can look confusing: live or recorded, group or private, Quran reading or Tajweed or Hifz. This guide walks through the decisions in the order you will actually face them.

It is written for families, so it covers practical questions as well as teaching ones: what equipment you need, what a realistic first month looks like, and how to read class prices.

What "online Quran classes" actually means

The phrase covers three quite different things, and mixing them up is the most common reason families end up disappointed.

  • Live one-to-one lessons. A teacher and one student on a video call. The teacher listens to the student recite and corrects it on the spot. This is the format Talqeen Academy teaches.
  • Live group classes. One teacher, several students. Cheaper, but each student gets less time reciting aloud, which is the part that matters most for Tajweed.
  • Recorded courses and apps. Useful for building a reading foundation or for practice between lessons, but nothing listens to you and corrects you. The Talqeen app, for example, is designed to build independent reading, with the Academy adding the live teacher on top.

Correct recitation is a listening skill. A teacher has to hear the mistake to fix it, which is why most families who want results choose live one-to-one lessons for the core of their learning.

What you need at home

  • A laptop, tablet or phone with a working camera, ideally on a stand so the teacher can see the student's face and the mushaf.
  • Headphones with a microphone. They reduce echo and make the teacher's corrections much clearer.
  • A printed mushaf or the book the teacher assigns, so the student is not tempted by other apps on the screen.
  • A quiet corner and a stable internet connection. A wired connection or sitting near the router helps more than a faster plan.

None of this needs to be expensive. Consistency of the setup matters more than quality of the equipment.

Which course to start with

Start from what the student can do today, not from what you hope they will do in a year.

  • Cannot yet read Arabic letters: start with Quran reading, which builds the alphabet, vowels and joining letters from the beginning.
  • Reads, but with mistakes or hesitation: add Tajweed so pronunciation and rules are corrected properly.
  • Reads well and wants to memorise: Hifz with a structured revision plan.
  • Wants to understand what they recite: Arabic, and later Islamic Studies.

If you are unsure, a short level check in the first lesson answers the question better than guessing.

What it costs and how to read the prices

Online classes are usually priced per hour, per class, or per month. Always convert to a per-hour figure so you can compare fairly. At the time of writing, Talqeen Academy charges $10 per hour regardless of the course, with 30-minute classes at $5, 45-minute classes at $7.50 and one-hour classes at $10. Monthly totals depend on how many days a week you study. The pricing page always has the current figures.

Class length should follow the student. Thirty minutes suits younger children and short revision sessions; 45 minutes is a good default for most students; a full hour suits adults and Hifz students who need time to revise as well as memorise.

What the first month should look like

  1. Week 1: a level check and a clear goal. The teacher listens to the student read and explains the starting point honestly.
  2. Week 2: the most frequent mistakes are named and corrected, with short homework.
  3. Week 3: a steady rhythm: revise, learn one new thing, correct, repeat.
  4. Week 4: a review with the parent or adult student: what improved, what still needs work, what the next month will focus on.

If after a month you cannot say what has improved, raise it with the teacher. A good academy will welcome that conversation.

Questions to ask before you enrol

  • Is there a free trial lesson, and is it with the teacher I would actually be studying with?
  • Are the lessons live and one-to-one, or recorded or group?
  • What are the teachers' qualifications, and do they hold an Ijazah for Quran teaching?
  • How will progress be reported to me?
  • What happens if we need to reschedule, or if we are not happy?
