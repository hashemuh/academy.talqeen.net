How Many Pages Are in the Quran? Editions, Ayahs and Study Planning

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 20265 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

The Quran's structure helps readers locate passages and plan study. Page numbers belong to a particular printed or digital layout, while surah and ayah references identify passages independently of the size of a book. This distinction matters when comparing copies or sharing an assignment.

The source gave numerical totals for pages, verses, words and letters, then introduced six course types. Those details are preserved here with qualifications about editions and counting methods rather than treating every total as a universal physical property.

How Many Pages Does a Mushaf Have?

A familiar Madani layout has 604 pages. Other formats may use different scripts, type sizes and line counts, so the Quran does not have one required page total in every edition. The source's statement that every juz has exactly twenty pages is also too absolute.

In a 604-page copy, the average is about 20.13 pages per juz, and actual boundaries should be checked. Many ordinary pages follow a consistent line layout, while opening or special pages may differ. A familiar format can support navigation without being universally ideal.

How Many Ayahs Are There?

The commonly printed Kufan count is 6,236. Recognised counting traditions differ in where verse divisions are counted; this does not mean their totals represent different amounts of Quranic content.

The source described 6,236 as excluding every basmalah and proposed 6,349 when including them. That simple addition is misleading: the familiar count already numbers the basmalah in Al-Fatihah, and basmalah also occurs within 27:30. Unnumbered opening formulas cannot simply be added to create a universally accepted alternate ayah total.

Opening basmalah appears before the surahs other than At-Tawbah, with the relevant recitation details taught separately. Ayahs vary greatly in length. 55:64 is a one-word example, whereas 2:282 is a long passage concerning debt documentation.

Surahs and Revelation Classification

There are 114 surahs. In a commonly used classification, Makkan concerns revelation before the Hijrah and Madinan after it; location alone is not the defining test. Individual passages and scholarly classifications can require further discussion.

The source associated Makkan material with faith, morals and the Hereafter, and Madinan material with community and legal matters. These are broad patterns, not rules that every chapter fits exclusively. See the surah guide.

Word and Letter Totals Need a Method

The source reported approximately 77,430 Arabic words and 323,670 letters. Keep those as source estimates rather than one method-independent result. Orthography, attached particles and what is treated as a written letter affect a count.

Exact numbers require a specified text and counting convention. Linguistic appreciation of the Quran should not be made dependent on an unexplained tally. Recitation encouragement concerning letters appears in Tirmidhi 2910, which distinguishes alif, lam and mim rather than treating their sequence as one letter.

The Longest and Shortest Surahs

Al-Baqarah, surah 2, has 286 ayahs and is the longest chapter. Its subjects include faith, worship, law, social responsibilities and accounts involving earlier communities and prophets.

Al-Kawthar, surah 108, is shortest in its brief overall wording and has three ayahs. Three verses alone are not a unique test: Al-Asr and An-Nasr also have three. Study its message through reliable explanation rather than a size comparison alone.

Ordering, Juz and Preservation

The mushaf is not a chronological sequence of revelation. Chapters may address multiple themes; that does not mean they lack coherence or that the entire text can be described as having no thematic structure. The thirty juz provide convenient recitation divisions, and one each day gives thirty daily portions.

Muslims understand the Quran as preserved revelation. Written copies and oral learning are central to its transmission. Recognised qiraat, orthographic conventions and verse-count traditions need to be understood within that history rather than erased by a claim that every printed feature has always been identical.

Six Distinct Course Goals

Use the following course categories to identify learning goals and compare syllabuses. Ask which subjects are covered, how reading is assessed and what practice will be expected between lessons.

  • Hifz: a plan for new memorisation, accurate reading, feedback and continuing revision.
  • Recitation: pronunciation, pace and fluency, with a pleasant voice serving the rules.
  • Tajweed: articulation, letter characteristics and rules applied in actual reading.
  • Noorani Qaida: foundational letters, vowel patterns and word reading for an assessed beginner.
  • Quranic Arabic: vocabulary, grammar and sentence relationships.
  • Tafsir: reliable explanation, context and interpretation rather than language guesses.

The original descriptions claimed certified tutors, proven methods and constant support. Ask what the actual instructor is qualified to teach and how progress is evaluated. See course-path comparison and completion planning.

Assess a Provider and Its Enrolment Process

The source promoted comprehensive courses, native Arab tutors, tailored lessons, flexible times, transparent fees and free trials. These are marketing descriptions to verify, not an independent ranking or Talqeen commitments.

It described requesting a trial, supplying name, email and course preference, arranging a time, attending the lesson, and then choosing a course and payment plan if satisfied. Confirm the process and terms actually used by your provider through course information; no source registration or payment link is needed in this guide.

Choose materials and support according to the actual learning need. A page count is useful for planning, but accurate reading and understanding require more than completing a numerical target.
