What Is an Ijazah in the Quran? Meaning, Sanad and Requirements

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

An Ijazah is an authorisation from a teacher within a defined area of learning. In Quran recitation, it can document that a student has completed the required reading and assessment in the recitation specified by the authorising teacher. Its value lies in the learning and oral verification behind it, not the appearance of a certificate alone.

The source discusses recitation and memorisation routes, the chain of teachers and preparation for teaching. Some of its descriptions are too absolute: an Ijazah is not a lifetime guarantee that the holder will never make a mistake, and every route does not necessarily have the same memorisation requirements.

What Does Ijazah Mean?

Ijazah means permission or authorisation. A qualified teacher hears the required recitation, corrects it and decides whether the student meets the relevant standard. The authorisation should identify its scope, including the riwayah or other material where relevant.

The source uses Ijazah and Sanad interchangeably, but they describe related concepts. The Ijazah is the authorisation; a Sanad is the chain through which the learning or recitation is transmitted. A certificate may record both, and the wording should be understood rather than assumed.

Quran teaching has been passed orally through generations, beginning with the Prophet, peace be upon him, and his companions. Where an Ijazah includes a connected Sanad, the teacher can explain the chain and the specific transmission it records.

Why Is Quran Ijazah Valued?

The process emphasises accurate recitation, applied Tajweed and direct assessment. It can motivate careful study and provide information about a teacher’s learning background. A student seeking to teach should understand the limits and responsibilities of the authorisation.

The source cites Sahih al-Bukhari 5027: “The best among you (Muslims) are those who learn the Qur’an and teach it.” It also recalls the Prophet’s request, “Recite the Qur’an to me,” and his wish to hear it from another person, recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5050.

These narrations support the importance of learning, teaching and attentive listening. They should not be presented as defining a modern certificate format or proving every advertised programme’s standards.

Two Learning Routes Described in the Source

1. Recitation with Tajweed

A recitation route assesses the relevant reading, articulation, characteristics and rules. Its scope may be one riwayah, such as Hafs from Asim, or a broader study of Qiraat. Hafs is a transmitted narration within Asim’s reading, not a separate version of the Quran.

The source lists four scope descriptions: one narration, seven Qiraat, three Qiraat and ten Qiraat. These should not be treated as universal certificate grades. In advanced study, “three” may refer to the additional canonical readings studied alongside the seven. Ask the teacher exactly what the programme covers.

  • One named narration, such as Hafs ‘an ‘Asim.
  • A specified study of the seven canonical readings.
  • A specified study of three additional readings where that is the intended scope.
  • A programme covering the ten canonical readings under its stated method.

Broader coverage is a broader scope, not an automatic ranking of every person’s overall teaching ability. Explore Talqeen Academy’s course options and ask about the actual recitation goal before enrolling.

2. Memorisation with assessed recitation

A memorisation route typically requires the relevant complete Quran recitation from memory with the teacher’s correction and assessment. It connects Hifz with accurate oral performance rather than treating memorised word count as sufficient.

The source says full memorisation is necessary for every Ijazah, yet also mentions reading from the Mushaf. Requirements differ by route and authorising teacher. A recitation programme may assess reading from the text, while a memorisation programme assesses recall. Confirm the standard directly instead of applying one route’s conditions to all others.

An online format can provide live listening, but delivery through a screen is not proof of qualification. Ask who grants the authorisation, what is assessed and how the teacher’s own relevant authority is established.

Preparation and Requirements to Discuss

The original checklist includes recitation, Tajweed, memorisation, oral instruction, stopping rules, articulation, Qiraat study and personal responsibility. Retain those topics while distinguishing general preparation from scope-specific requirements.

  • Relevant recitation: identify the reading or narration in which you will be assessed.
  • Tajweed: understand and apply the rules required by the teacher’s programme.
  • Memorisation: establish whether the particular authorisation requires complete recall or permits reading from the Mushaf.
  • Authorising teacher: study with someone qualified to assess and authorise the specified material.
  • Waqf and restarting: learn appropriate stopping and restarting within the recitation.
  • Articulation: produce the letters and their characteristics accurately.
  • Qiraat scope: study what the chosen authorisation covers; all ten readings are not a universal prerequisite for a single-narration route.
  • Responsibility and conduct: recognise the trust involved in transmitting knowledge, practise good conduct and remain open to correction.

Do not use a checklist as a substitute for the teacher’s actual assessment. Ask how errors are handled and when a student is considered ready.

Must Every Quran Teacher Have an Ijazah?

The source answers that Ijazah is recommended rather than required for all teaching. This needs qualification: the level and subject of instruction matter, and no one should teach beyond their competence or claim an authorisation they do not hold.

For example, SeekersGuidance’s answer on teaching without Ijazah distinguishes competent teaching of a commonly accepted recitation from teaching specialised readings that require direct qualification. Discuss your intended role with a knowledgeable teacher rather than treating the source’s simple “no” as unlimited permission.

The source also calls an online academy the easiest route and advises checking accreditation or recognition. Ease cannot be guaranteed, and terms such as “accredited” need an identified body and scope. Investigate the authorising teacher, assessment and certificate wording first.

Look for the Learning Behind the Authorisation

An Ijazah can mark a significant stage in Quran study when its scope, teacher and assessment are clear. It does not remove the need for revision, careful practice or responsible teaching. A beautiful certificate alone cannot establish those qualities.

The source promotes experienced tutors and courses for children and adults. Assess actual teaching rather than a claim that a provider is the “best.” Continue with becoming a Hafiz and Qari and Hafiz roles to choose a suitable next stage.
