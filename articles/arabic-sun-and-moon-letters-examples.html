Arabic Sun and Moon Letters: Rules, Chart and Quran Examples

Talqeen Academy · Updated 10 October 20265 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00) · Arabic

Arabic sun and moon letters explain what happens to the lam in the definite article ال, often corresponding to “the” in English. The familiar alphabet classification has twenty-eight letters, divided into two groups of fourteen.

Recognising the next letter helps you pronounce the article accurately. The rule concerns assimilation, not whether a word has a sunny or moon-related meaning.

What Changes in Pronunciation?

Before a sun letter, the article's lam assimilates into the following consonant. That consonant is doubled, shown by shadda in vocalised text. الشَّمْس is therefore read ash-shams, rather than al-shams.

Before a moon letter, lam remains audible and the article does not cause doubling of the next consonant. الْقَمَر is read al-qamar. The source mistakenly described ash-shams as retaining lam in one explanation; its correct contrast is assimilation versus a clearly pronounced lam.

The labels come from shams, sun, beginning with ش, and qamar, moon, beginning with ق. A shadda means consonant doubling, not simply extra stress or a louder voice.

Definite and Indefinite Nouns

Nakirah means an indefinite noun; marifah means a definite noun. These are categories of nouns, not two separate Arabic article words. Arabic has no ordinary standalone indefinite article corresponding directly to English “a” or “an”.

مُدَرِّسٌ can mean a teacher, while الْمُدَرِّسُ means the teacher. Tanwin often accompanies an indefinite declinable noun, but it is not a universal test: not every indefinite form has tanwin, and proper names may have it. Likewise, ال is one way of making a noun definite, not the only way.

Applying the Two Rules

The sun letters are ت ث د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ل ن. In النَّاس, an-nas, lam assimilates and nun is doubled. Avoid adding a separate lam sound before it.

The moon letters are أ ب ج ح خ ع غ ف ق ك م ه و ي. In الْبَيْت, al-bayt, lam remains clear. A moon-letter word may contain another shadda for a different reason; this rule says the article does not create doubling of the initial moon consonant.

The article also begins with hamzat al-wasl. Beginning a word and joining it to the preceding word therefore involve another rule, explained in the qat–wasl distinction.

Examples from the Quran

  • الشَّمْس in Yasin 36:38 illustrates shadda on shin.
  • الصَّالِحِينَ illustrates sad assimilation. The source cited 2:177, but that word appears in Al-Baqarah 2:130; 2:177 contains الصَّابِرِينَ, a different word that also begins with sun-letter sad.
  • النَّار in Al-Baqarah 2:24 illustrates doubled nun.
  • الْقَمَر in Al-Qamar 54:1 retains lam before qaf.
  • الْحَمْد in Al-Fatihah 1:2 retains lam before ha.
  • الْبَيْت in Quraysh 106:3 retains lam before ba.

Remembering and Practising the Letters

Use shams and qamar as memory cues, then practise the complete lists with familiar words. Identify ال in a passage, classify the following letter, listen to reliable audio and recite the phrase for feedback.

Vocalised Quran print often provides helpful marks, but learn the rule rather than relying on one font convention. Quran Academy's al- lesson provides a focused explanation. See also the Arabic alphabet.

The source promoted Egyptian tutors and Quranic Arabic classes. Nationality does not establish teaching competence; check the actual syllabus through course information. Careful application supports fluency without making this single rule sufficient for complete comprehension.
