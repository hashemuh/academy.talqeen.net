How to Revert to Islam: Shahada, First Steps and Returning to Faith

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

A person enters Islam through sincere belief in Allah alone and in Muhammad ﷺ as His Messenger, expressed in the declaration of faith. The central step is the Shahada, not completing a course, changing a name or arranging an elaborate ceremony. Learning and supported practice follow that commitment.

This guide also addresses Muslims who are returning to religious practice after a period of distance. Their situation should not automatically be treated as conversion: neglecting practice, uncertainty and an actual rejection of belief are different matters. A knowledgeable scholar can help resolve an individual case without rushing to label the person.

What “revert to Islam” means

Many Muslims use “revert” because Islamic teaching describes human beings as born upon fitrah, a natural disposition. In that usage, embracing Islam is described as returning to an original orientation towards God. “Convert” is also a common descriptive term in English; using that word does not invalidate a sincere declaration.

People approach this decision through study, personal reflection, conversations or a wish to return to beliefs they once held. Popularity and population growth should not be the basis for accepting a religion. Take time to understand its central claims and make the decision freely.

The Shahada and its two testimonies

The declaration affirms that no one is worthy of worship except Allah and that Muhammad ﷺ is His Messenger. Both parts matter: the first affirms God's oneness, and the second accepts the Prophet's message and guidance.

لَا إِلٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ مُحَمَّدٌ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ

Transliteration: Lā ilāha illā Allāh, Muḥammadun rasūlu Allāh.

A fuller testimony is: “I bear witness that there is no god worthy of worship except Allah, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah.” These words need sincere understanding and conviction. Reciting a formula without believing it is not the same as embracing its meaning.

If Arabic is difficult, express the testimony in a language you understand while learning the Arabic wording. Witnesses and a mosque setting can provide support, but they do not create belief in place of your own conviction. See the Arabic and English Shahada guide.

Seven practical steps for beginning

1. Clarify your intention

Ask whether you believe the message and wish to commit sincerely to Allah. Sahih al-Bukhari 1 places intention at the heart of actions. A decision made under pressure or simply to please another person should not be confused with a personal conviction.

2. Understand the testimony

You do not need scholarly expertise before taking the Shahada. You do need to understand the oneness of Allah and the messengership of Muhammad ﷺ. Further study will deepen that foundation. Questions about unfamiliar practices can be discussed as you learn rather than requiring complete mastery in advance.

3. State it sincerely

Choose a setting where you can speak calmly and freely, either alone or with someone you trust. Say both testimonies with understanding. If you want guided support, an imam or knowledgeable teacher can explain the words before you repeat them. A support appointment should not become a claim that entry into Islam requires institutional permission.

4. Learn purification and perform ghusl

The source presented ghusl after conversion as an obligation of the majority without qualification. Scholars differ over whether conversion itself makes the bath obligatory or recommended; other reasons for obligatory purification may also apply. This scholarly discussion identifies that difference. It is not a prerequisite for saying the Shahada.

Learn a complete method from a teacher: intend purification, remove impurities and ensure water reaches the body, with mouth and nose guidance according to the school followed. A normal shower needs the relevant intention and method to serve as ritual purification. Learn wudu as well, because it is part of your ongoing preparation for prayer.

5. Begin learning the five prayers

Learn prayer times, movements, purification and the recitations in manageable steps. Begin with what you can properly perform and obtain clear guidance on temporary accommodations while learning. Gradual learning does not mean that all obligations are indefinitely suspended.

6. Share the news when appropriate

Witnessing the declaration at a mosque can introduce you to people who will answer questions and offer practical help. Public announcement is not a condition of entry into Islam. Consider your circumstances when deciding whom to tell, especially if disclosure could create danger. Read more about witnesses.

7. Find dependable knowledge and community

A structured beginner course and considerate Muslim companions can help turn an initial commitment into a sustainable practice. The source cites Abu Dawud 4833 concerning the influence of close friends. Seek people who combine sound teaching with patience, and who encourage respectful relationships rather than unnecessary isolation.

What follows the Shahada spiritually and practically

Sahih Muslim 121 teaches that embracing Islam wipes away what preceded it. This is a source of hope, not permission to ignore responsibilities towards people. Questions about debts, property or rights should be addressed carefully.

Forgiveness, good deeds and repentance

The source attributed a lengthy wording about recording previous good deeds to Bukhari 41. That report describes multiplied rewards after sincere acceptance of Islam; Bukhari 1436 addresses prior good deeds. These teachings should be cited accurately rather than merged into a single quotation.

The source's numbers of ten to seven hundred concern the reward described in the report, not a financial account or a guaranteed outcome from every particular action. Treat forgiveness and reward with gratitude, and continue learning the conditions and responsibilities of good conduct.

How many people embrace Islam?

The source supplied five demographic claims: growth of about 347 million Muslims from 2010 to 2020; 20,000–30,000 US conversions per year; roughly 5,000 UK conversions annually with more than 60% women; a US convert share of 2–3%; and a projection that Islam could become the largest religion before the end of this century. These figures should not all be treated as current, comparable or verified counts.

Population growth is different from conversion. Historical estimates, annual flows, survey shares and long-term projections measure different things. The quoted US annual figure and UK figures lack a dated underlying source in the supplied article, so they are not dependable present-day totals.

The US percentage also needs correction: Pew's 2025 religious-switching analysis reports 20% of US Muslim adults as raised outside Islam, with 17% raised Christian and 3% raised in other backgrounds. That is a survey-based share, not an annual conversion count. Projections are conditional models, not predictions that can be guaranteed.

Returning after years away from practice

A Muslim who has become distant from prayer or community should not assume that the way back is closed. Quran 39:53 calls people who have transgressed not to despair of Allah's mercy. Make sincere repentance and begin taking practical steps towards worship and better conduct.

The source's statement that no sin can ever affect a person's religious status is too sweeping, as is treating every absence of practice identically. Scholars distinguish belief, explicit rejection and particular actions, and differ on some questions about abandoned prayer. Do not diagnose yourself from a general web article or label another person without knowledge.

Questions about names, family and independence

A new Muslim ordinarily does not need to replace a birth name with an Arabic one. Names with religiously problematic meanings may need individual guidance. An optional chosen name should not be mistaken for a universal condition of conversion.

You can embrace Islam while living with non-Muslim relatives. Maintain kindness and family ties, choose a calm moment for discussion and allow others time to respond. Their eventual acceptance cannot be guaranteed; patient behaviour is still worthwhile. A woman makes her own declaration and does not require a male guardian's permission to believe.

The testimony requires sincere acceptance, but this does not mean that every passing worry or unanswered question proves the absence of faith. Discuss real doubts honestly. Do not recite solely because someone pressures you to say words you do not yet accept.
