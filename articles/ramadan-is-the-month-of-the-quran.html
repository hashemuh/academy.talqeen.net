Ramadan: The Month of the Quran and a 30-Day Reading Plan

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

Ramadan's connection with the Quran invites Muslims to read, learn, reflect and apply its guidance. A reading plan can help organise the month, but completing pages should not displace attention to pronunciation, meaning or the worship obligations of everyday life.

Al-Baqarah 2:185 identifies Ramadan as the month of Quran revelation and describes its guidance and distinction between right and wrong. This connection explains the familiar description “the month of the Quran”.

Reading, Learning and Worship in Ramadan

The source cited the virtue of learning and teaching the Quran, recorded in Bukhari, and the guidance described in Al-Isra 17:9. These provide a basis for purposeful study rather than a promise that finishing one schedule ensures every desired outcome.

Bukhari 1899 describes the opening of heaven's gates, closure of Hell's gates and chaining of devils when Ramadan begins. Bukhari 1901 connects fasting in faith and hope of reward with forgiveness. These devotional reports should be presented with their conditions, not turned into a claim that everyone simply “pays for deeds” or achieves effortless success.

The source emphasised mercy, forgiveness, spiritual benefit, social connection and competition in good deeds. Retain that encouragement through sincere worship, learning and service. It did not substantiate a fixed numerical multiplier for every action, so none is assigned here.

A Practical Daily Reading Pattern

The Quran is divided into thirty juz. Reading one juz each day gives a thirty-day completion plan. In a common 604-page edition, a juz averages about twenty pages but is not always exactly twenty. The source's page method therefore needs adjustment to the actual juz boundaries.

Its instruction to read four pages “a day after each Salah” meant approximately four pages after each of the five daily prayers: about twenty pages per day. Another version divided the same amount into two pages before and two after each prayer. These are alternative ways of distributing the target, not additional compulsory reading.

Check the remaining pages and ayahs rather than stopping automatically at twenty. Ramadan can last twenty-nine days, so a thirty-day table needs an adjustment if you want to finish within a shorter month. Keep a modest buffer or divide remaining work according to your actual ability.

Why Laylat al-Qadr Matters

Al-Qadr 97:1–5 links revelation with the Night of Decree, describes it as better than a thousand months, and mentions the angels and Spirit descending by Allah's permission, with peace until dawn. These meanings encourage worship and reflection without specifying a night from this table.

The source also described the beginning of revelation in the cave of Hira, when Jibril brought the opening verses of Al-Alaq. Bukhari 3 records that beginning. Al-Alaq 96:1–5 speaks of reading in the Lord's name, creation, teaching by the pen and knowledge given to humanity.

Keeping the Plan Meaningful

Ask a teacher to correct unclear reading, include time for translation or tafsir, and reduce the page goal if accuracy suffers. See edition and page-count guidance. A schedule supports worship; it is not a measure of every person's sincerity.

The source encouraged sharing the plan with friends and family and seeking reward by helping others read. Share an accurate explanation and let people adapt it to their needs. Its final invitation promoted Mishkah discounts and trials; no source booking links or transferred offers are included here.

The Quran and the Prophet's example provide a continuing basis for guidance beyond Ramadan. Keep a sustainable reading and learning habit after the month rather than treating completion as the end of the relationship.
