Isra and Miraj: The Night Journey, Ascension and Lessons of Prayer

Talqeen Academy · Updated 10 October 20265 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00) · Islamic Studies

Isra and Miraj describes the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ miraculous night journey and ascension. Isra refers to the journey from Al-Masjid Al-Haram to Al-Masjid Al-Aqsa; Miraj refers to the ascent described in the hadith accounts. Muslims study the event for its teachings about prayer, revelation, mercy and accountability.

The Quran states the Night Journey in 17:1, and authentic reports provide further details. A responsible account distinguishes those texts from popular additions, disputed dates and symbolic expressions that are not themselves prophetic quotations.

Isra: the Night Journey

Quran 17:1 glorifies Allah, who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to Al-Aqsa, whose surroundings He blessed, to show him signs. The verse establishes the journey's religious significance without providing every detail of the later narrative.

Sahih Muslim 162a, narrated from Anas ibn Malik, describes Buraq as a white animal larger than a donkey and smaller than a mule. The source incorrectly names Abu Hurayrah as the narrator of that reference. Its further description of wings should not be attributed to this report, which does not establish that feature.

The supplied article says the journey took place “in an instant.” It was miraculous, but the verse identifies a night journey rather than specifying a measured duration of one instant. Avoid adding precision not found in the cited text.

The accounts of the Prophet praying in Jerusalem and leading the prophets underline the connection with earlier messengers. Al-Aqsa's religious importance is not dependent on the date of a later building. Its status as the earlier qiblah belongs to the history of prayer; the journey did not itself make it the first qiblah for the first time.

Miraj: the ascension and the seven heavens

The reports describe Jibril accompanying the Prophet through the heavens, with permission sought at their gates and meetings with earlier prophets. The familiar sequence is:

  1. First heaven: Adam.
  2. Second heaven: Yahya and Isa.
  3. Third heaven: Yusuf.
  4. Fourth heaven: Idris.
  5. Fifth heaven: Harun.
  6. Sixth heaven: Musa.
  7. Seventh heaven: Ibrahim.

These meetings are part of a revealed account of the unseen, not a map that can be reduced to ordinary travel measurements. Respect the wording of the reports and avoid speculative details about the physical mechanisms.

Sidrat al-Muntaha and the Quranic signs

Quran 53:13–18 mentions a further seeing, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, the Garden of Refuge and great signs of the Lord. Interpret these verses through reliable Tafsir alongside the hadith accounts rather than assuming that every phrase answers every theological question about the ascension.

The source describes Sidrat al-Muntaha as a boundary beyond which no creation can pass, yet also describes the Prophet's exceptional journey. That blanket wording needs care. State the named boundary and the honour described in the texts without making an unsupported universal claim about all created beings.

Descriptions of divine communication must not imply that Allah is contained in a place or that the journey proves a particular unqualified account of physically seeing Him. Scholars discuss relevant reports and interpretations; a beginner narrative should not erase those distinctions.

The five daily prayers

The accounts describe fifty prayers initially enjoined, with the Prophet returning to seek a reduction following Musa's advice, until five daily prayers remained with the reward described as fifty. This is a central lesson about the importance of Salah and Allah's mercy.

The source cites Bukhari 349, Muslim 162 and Bukhari 7517. Their wording and chains should be kept clear: Bukhari 7517 is narrated from Anas, not Abu Hurayrah as the source states. The obligation is five daily prayers, not an optional reduction that a person may negotiate again.

Prayer provides regular occasions for worship, remembrance and repentance. Calling it a person's “spiritual lifeline” is a helpful metaphor, but describing every Salah as a literal personal Miraj should not be presented as an authenticated hadith without evidence.

The historical setting and return to Makkah

The source places the event during the Year of Sorrow, associated with the deaths of Khadijah and Abu Talib and the hardship of rejection at Taif. These experiences form part of the broader pre-Hijrah biography. The exact date and placement of Isra and Miraj are disputed, so it should not be stated that all accounts fix it within that same year.

The story is often taught as reassurance after hardship. That lesson does not establish a promise that every distress will end immediately or in a miraculous experience. Faith and patience can continue while difficulties remain.

Accounts describe Quraysh's response when the Prophet related the journey. Abu Bakr's affirmation is well known in discussions of his title Al-Siddiq. The supplied reference to Bukhari 3598 is incorrect: that report concerns Zaynab bint Jahsh and the barrier of Yajuj and Majuj, not the quoted affirmation. A familiar story still needs its proper source and assessment.

Accountability and reports of punishment

Abu Dawud 4878 relates the image of people scratching their faces and chests with copper nails, associated with attacking others' honour. Its moral warning concerns backbiting and harm through speech.

The source also lists neglect of prayer and consuming interest among scenes witnessed during the journey. Those additional details require their own reports and grading; they cannot all be assigned to the backbiting reference. Preserve the distinction between separate narratives rather than blending them into one authenticated description.

Five themes in the event's significance

  • Prophethood: the journey belongs to the signs and honour associated with the Prophet's mission.
  • Prayer: the five daily prayers remain an enduring practical duty.
  • Reassurance: the event offers a theme of hope within a difficult period.
  • The prophets' shared message: the narrative connects Muhammad ﷺ with earlier messengers and worship of Allah.
  • Moral responsibility: reports concerning the unseen remind readers of accountability.

Ten lessons for everyday life

1. Give Salah its place

Learn the prayer correctly and protect its times. The journey's lesson is strongest when it changes daily worship, not only an annual gathering.

2. Practise patience during hardship

Read the event alongside the Prophet's wider biography. Seek support and remain responsible rather than treating patience as passive refusal to address a difficulty.

3. Respect the unity of the prophets' message

The meetings and prayer connect the narrative with earlier revelation. Study that continuity without inventing identical legal details for every prophetic community.

4. Recognise Al-Aqsa's significance

Learn its place in Islamic worship and history, and speak about the sacred site accurately and respectfully. Its importance should not be reduced to a decorative background for the story.

5. Connect spiritual learning with conduct

Reflection on revelation and the Hereafter should lead towards honesty, care for others and accountability. Emotional inspiration alone is not the entire lesson.

6. Understand faith without rejecting reason

The source labels this lesson “faith over rationality.” Believing a miracle reported in revelation is not a command to abandon careful thought or accept every unsupported story. Investigating a report's authenticity is part of responsible study.

7. Remember mercy

The reduction from fifty prayers to five highlights mercy in the transmitted account. It does not remove the duties that remain or guarantee that every personal burden will be immediately lifted.

8. Take the Hereafter seriously

Study sound reports about rewards and consequences. Avoid attributing every vivid popular punishment scene to the same authentic narration.

9. Strengthen worship and dua

Prayer and supplication allow regular remembrance of Allah. A feeling of closeness may accompany them, but no particular emotion is required for worship to be meaningful.

10. Develop conviction through knowledge

The response to the journey offers a theme of trust in revelation. Ground conviction in reliable texts and their explanation rather than pressure to suppress genuine questions.

Eight story-based reflections

  1. Buraq: study the transmitted description and distinguish it from later artistic additions. The lesson of trust does not require inventing wings.
  2. Meeting the prophets: reflect on the shared call to worship Allah while keeping each prophet's place in the narrative clear.
  3. The prayer obligation: connect the story to the five prayers you perform rather than regarding it only as an extraordinary event.
  4. Scenes of consequences: use verified reports to examine your conduct, particularly harm caused by speech.
  5. Abu Bakr's response: learn the correct source before sharing an attributed statement.
  6. Sidrat al-Muntaha: respect the limits of knowledge about the unseen and avoid speculative geography.
  7. Hardship in the biography: recognise the theme of resilience while acknowledging that the precise event date is disputed.
  8. Mercy in the prayer reduction: value the five remaining obligations and the reward described in the report.

Commemoration, worship and disputed dates

Some communities hold lectures, Quran readings or gatherings to discuss the event. Scholars differ over annual commemorative practices, and the popular date of 27 Rajab is not an undisputed historically established date. State those distinctions instead of treating one custom as universal.

Voluntary worship, repentance and dua are valuable generally. The supplied article does not establish a special guaranteed reward, a fixed number of prayers or a unique annual ritual for this night. Do not transfer the virtue of the historical event automatically into an invented observance.

Educational programmes can explain the story and its references without making attendance a condition of faith. Preserve the daily obligation of Salah and the continuing study of the Quran as the practical focus.
