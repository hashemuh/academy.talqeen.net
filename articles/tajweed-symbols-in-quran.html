Tajweed Symbols in the Quran: Vowels, Stops and Reading Marks

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 20265 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

Marks around the Quran's Arabic letters help readers identify vowels, consonant doubling, long sounds and suitable stopping places. Other signs mark ayah endings, divisions and prostration passages. They support recitation, but a printed sign does not replace learning the sound from a qualified teacher.

Mushaf editions use different notation conventions. Consult the legend of the edition you read. A hizb is a division of a juz, conventionally half a juz, not “half a chapter” as the source stated. Surahs vary in length and do not correspond to equal hizb divisions.

What These Marks Do

Vowel marks tell you how a consonant is read; stopping signs guide waqf and restarting; other symbols highlight particular recitation instructions. Recognising the category helps you avoid treating every small mark as the same kind of command.

The source discussed twelve signs. Its English vowel comparisons and several examples needed correction. The explanations below retain the subjects while distinguishing pronunciation from stopping and avoiding rules that do not apply to every printed Quran.

Twelve Signs and Their Uses

1. Continuation Preferred: صلى

This sign commonly indicates that stopping is allowed but joining is preferable. The source named ربهم as an example without a verse reference. That isolated word cannot identify a specific marked location; find the complete ayah in your own mushaf before applying the example.

2. Do Not Stop Here: لا

In a common stopping-sign system, لا indicates a place where an intentional stop should be avoided. If breath or necessity forces a stop, learn where to restart so the meaning remains connected. Do not interpret the mark as an instruction to keep reading beyond your comfortable breath.

3. Stopping Preferred: قلى

This commonly permits joining while preferring a stop. The source example comes from Al-Kahf 18:22:

قُلْ رَبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِمْ مَا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ ۗ فَلَا تُمَارِ فِيهِمْ

It highlights the place after قَلِيلٌ. The excerpt is not the full ayah, and the displayed symbol should be compared with your edition's legend.

4. Necessary Stop: م

Waqf lazim is commonly shown by a small mim or a corresponding Quranic stop mark. It warns that joining may suggest an unintended relationship in meaning. “Necessary” is the notation category; detailed stopping guidance should be learned with the passage. The source used part of Al-An'am 6:36:

إِنَّمَا يَسْتَجِيبُ الَّذِينَ يَسْمَعُونَ ۘ وَالْمَوْتَى يَبْعَثُهُمُ اللَّهُ

The marked place follows يَسْمَعُونَ. Learn the sound and restart from a teacher rather than relying on an unusual font rendering.

5. Permissible Stop: ج

This commonly allows stopping or continuing. The source example, from Al-Kahf 18:13, marks the place after بِالْحَقِّ:

نَحْنُ نَقُصُّ عَلَيْكَ نَبَأَهُمْ بِالْحَقِّ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ فِتْيَةٌ آمَنُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ

The excerpt illustrates the location but does not contain the entire ayah. A teacher can explain appropriate breathing and joining in its full context.

6. Madd or Prolongation Mark

A wavy mark can indicate an extended sound. It does not mean every occurrence is two counts: duration depends on the type of madd and the reading taught. The source excerpt from Al-Ma'idah 5:2 includes آمِّينَ, a madd lazim example taught with six counts in Hafs:

وَلَا آمِّينَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ وَرِضْوَانًا

Ask for the relevant rule instead of using a generic “stretch this” instruction.

7. Fathah: بَ

A small stroke above a consonant indicates the short a vowel. In كَتَبَ, the first syllable is ka. English “cat” is only an approximate comparison and can encourage the wrong vowel quality; listen to an Arabic model.

8. Dammah: بُ

The small curved mark above a consonant indicates a short u vowel, with appropriately rounded lips. It is a vowel sign shaped like a small waw, not a separate long waw to pronounce after every consonant.

9. Kasrah: بِ

A stroke below a consonant indicates a short i vowel. In كِتَاب, the beginning is ki. Jaw movement alone does not define the sound; the written sign and a spoken model should be learned together.

10. Sukun

Sukun indicates that a consonant has no following short vowel. The source's example مَلْك has a sakin lam. This does not mean pause between every consonant and the next one. Some mushaf systems use round or other-shaped marks whose conventions need explanation.

11. Tanwin

Fathatan, kasratan and dammatan commonly represent an, in and un endings when joined, subject to Tajweed. The source's examples are corrected to كِتَابًا, كِتَابٍ and كِتَابٌ. Stopping changes some endings, so joined pronunciation cannot be applied unchanged at every stop. Tanwin often marks indefiniteness, but that is not its only grammatical function.

12. Shaddah

Shaddah marks consonant doubling: a sakin consonant followed by the same consonant with a vowel. In مَشَّى, it is the shin, not the mim, that carries shaddah; an approximate rendering is mash-sha. Doubling is not the same as making every letter heavy or adding arbitrary length.

Using Symbols in Real Reading

Learn the legend, practise a short passage and have the result checked. See Tajweed rules and letter foundations. Symbols help preserve accurate reading but cannot by themselves guarantee correct meaning or prevent every inappropriate pause.

Learn Tajweed symbols through examples from the reading tradition and Mushaf you use. Ask a teacher to demonstrate the relevant rule, then practise it in words and passages rather than memorising labels alone. Related guidance: course information.
