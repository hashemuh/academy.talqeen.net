The Six Pillars of Iman: Belief and Careful Explanations

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

The six pillars of Iman are belief in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day and divine decree. They are described in the hadith of Jibril, alongside explanations of Islam and Ihsan.

Iman concerns faith; a linguistic relationship with security does not promise immunity from hardship or danger. Do not confuse six pillars of belief with the five pillars of Islamic practice.

1. Belief in Allah

This includes recognising Allah's existence, lordship, exclusive right to worship, and names and attributes. The source's “belief in His servitude” should mean worship directed to Him, not servitude belonging to Allah.

Surah Al-Ikhlas teaches divine oneness and incomparability:

قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ۝ اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ ۝ لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ ۝ وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ

The reference for “nothing like Him” is Quran 42:11, not 24:11 as stated in the source. The Creator is not comparable to creation, does not need creation and alone deserves worship.

The source used theological wording about bodies, organs, place and time. Scholars explain divine attributes through differing creedal formulations; those phrases should not be passed off as a verbatim Quran statement or an identical formulation used by every school. Learn them with reliable guidance while retaining the foundational rejection of likeness and partners.

The source also emphasised creation's dependence, divine commands and the sending of revelation. Affirming these does not mean humans know every divine wisdom or that all unseen matters have been disclosed.

2. Belief in Angels

Angels are created servants, not divine beings. Revelation describes their obedience and worship, including Quran 21:19 and 66:6. The source also drew on Quran 4:136 concerning belief in Allah, angels, revelation and the Last Day.

The report of angels created from light is in Muslim 2996. Descriptions such as Jibril's six hundred wings and the seventy thousand angels entering Al-Bayt al-Ma'mur belong to reported unseen knowledge, not imaginative physical speculation.

  • Jibril: revelation to the prophets. The source listed Gabriel and Jibril twice; they refer to the same angel.
  • Mikail: named in revelation, with traditionally discussed tasks concerning provision.
  • Israfil: associated with the trumpet and named in prophetic supplication.
  • Munkar and Nakir: named in reports concerning questioning in the grave.
  • The Angel of Death: the Quranic designation. “Izrael” in the source is not established as that name by an authentic Quran or prophetic text.
  • Malik: named regarding Hell's keeper.
  • Ridwan: a commonly circulated name for Paradise's keeper, requiring evidence rather than presentation as equally authenticated.
  • Raqib and Atid: descriptors in Quran 50:18; not evidence for two proper names separately assigned good and bad deeds.

Other described roles include recording, guardianship, bearing the Throne and keeping Paradise or Hell. Believe in what reliable revelation establishes without inventing names or the total number. The source's broad recording theme is sound, while details require careful evidence.

3. Belief in Revealed Books

Muslim belief recognises revelation sent to prophets as guidance. Named examples include Ibrahim's scriptures, Dawud's Zabur, Musa's Tawrah, Isa's Injil and the Quran revealed to Muhammad.

Distinguish original revelation from every later edition or interpretation of a scripture. The Quran is the final revelation in Islamic belief, and the Sunnah explains practice; saying “follow only the Quran” should not be used to discard authentic prophetic guidance.

The source referred to earlier teachings not abrogated. Their relevance is determined through Islamic scholarship, not by independently choosing rules from different texts. What has not been named in revelation need not be invented.

4. Belief in Messengers

Muslims affirm the prophets and messengers sent by Allah. Quran 16:36 describes messengers calling to worship Allah and avoid false worship. Uncertainty about an unsupported biography should not be treated as denial of a prophet.

The familiar twenty-five names are Adam, Idris, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Ibrahim, Lut, Isma'il, Ishaq, Ya'qub, Yusuf, Ayyub, Shu'ayb, Musa, Harun, Dhul-Kifl, Dawud, Sulayman, Ilyas, Al-Yasa', Yunus, Zakariyya, Yahya, Isa and Muhammad.

The source's Biblical identifications of Hud as Eber, Salih as Shaloh, Shu'ayb as Jethro and Dhul-Kifl as Ezekiel are not all established correspondences. Nor should every named prophet be described as mentioned in the Bible. Its total of 124,000 requires report assessment and should not be presented as an agreed verified census.

Every messenger is a prophet in the usual distinction, while scholars explain differences between the terms in various ways. The source's new-law versus previous-law definition is one simplified account, not a universally sufficient rule.

5. Belief in the Last Day

Belief includes resurrection, gathering, accountability, the scales, the bridge, Paradise and Hell as established by revelation. Quran 21:47 emphasises justice even for the smallest deed.

The source listed names such as Judgement, Resurrection, Reckoning, Warning, Loss and Gain, Emergence, Recompense, Eternity, Gathering, Regret, the Deafening Blast, Great Catastrophe, Overwhelming Event, Inevitable Event, Reality and Calamity. Study their actual Quranic contexts rather than assume every English label is one fixed official title.

Judgement is not a simple human arithmetic algorithm deciding every person's destination. Allah's justice, mercy, faith and other revealed teachings matter. The source's assertion that everyone entering Hell necessarily remains eternally overlooks teachings about sinful believers and should not stand as an unqualified rule.

6. Belief in Divine Decree

Quran 54:49–50 and 22:70 concern decree and knowledge. A common four-part explanation is knowledge, writing, will and creation. The source repeated knowledge and omitted creation from its four-item account; this scholarly explanation supplies the distinction.

Belief in the Preserved Tablet does not eliminate responsibility or the need to act. Divine will should not be confused with approval of every wrongdoing. The source's claim that decree began only after Adam's creation was inaccurate.

Qada and qadar have contextual and scholarly usages, and the source's sharp qadar-versus-qadr English division was unreliable. Study the Night of Decree and daily decree without inventing a universal vocabulary formula. Quran 4:79 also requires context alongside discussion of decree and accountability.

The source cited Ibn Majah 85 on faith and decree. Use it with proper report assessment and the clearer six-pillar framework in the hadith of Jibril.

Belief and Continuing Learning

Strengthening faith can guide worship and conduct without guaranteeing freedom from grief, illness or insecurity. Read the pillars of practice, Remembering Allah’s Names: Meanings, Dua and Spiritual Benefits and How to Learn Islam from the Beginning: 12 Foundational Steps, or consult course details.

Prophet Names and English Study Materials

English resources commonly use Noah for Nuh, Abraham for Ibrahim, Lot for Lut, Ishmael for Isma'il, Isaac for Ishaq, Jacob for Ya'qub, Joseph for Yusuf and Job for Ayyub. Musa is commonly rendered Moses, Harun Aaron, Dawud David, Sulayman Solomon, Ilyas Elijah, Al-Yasa' Elisha, Yunus Jonah, Zakariyya Zechariah, Yahya John and Isa Jesus. Adam and Muhammad are usually recognisable across the two naming conventions.

Idris is sometimes identified with Enoch, but that identification should be assessed alongside the other uncertain correspondences already noted. Hud, Salih, Shu'ayb and Dhul-Kifl should not be assigned an English Biblical identity simply because a comparison chart does so. A spelling correspondence helps readers follow a discussion; it does not establish that every later account about that figure is part of Islamic revelation.

Study the six articles together while keeping their subjects distinct. Belief in angels concerns created servants and their revealed roles; belief in books concerns revelation; belief in messengers concerns the people entrusted with conveying it. The Last Day concerns resurrection and accountability, while decree concerns Allah's knowledge, will and creation alongside human responsibility.

When a question goes beyond what is clearly established, distinguish a Quranic statement, an assessed hadith, a scholar's explanation and a popular story. This avoids presenting an uncertain name or numerical report with the same confidence as the central articles of faith. Keep a list of questions and review them with someone qualified to explain both the evidence and its limits.
