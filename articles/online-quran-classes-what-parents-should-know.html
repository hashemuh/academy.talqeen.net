What Parents Should Know Before Starting Online Quran Classes

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 00:26:28 (UTC+03:00)

Choosing a good teacher is half the picture. The other half is what happens at home: where the lesson takes place, how you talk about it, and how you respond when your child is tired or frustrated.

This article focuses on the parent's side of online Quran classes: what to set up, what to watch for, and how to help without turning lessons into a battle.

Your role: supporter, not second teacher

You do not need to know Tajweed or Arabic to help your child succeed. Your job is to protect the routine and show that the lesson matters.

  • Make sure the child is on time, fed and has used the bathroom before class.
  • Sit nearby for the first few lessons, then step back as the child gets comfortable.
  • Ask "What did your teacher help you with today?" rather than "Did you do well?"
  • Leave correction to the teacher. If you fix every mistake at home, children start to hide them.

Set up a safe, calm lesson space

  • Use a shared area of the home rather than a closed bedroom, with the door open if the lesson is in a separate room.
  • Keep the device on a stand at eye level so the teacher sees the child and the mushaf clearly.
  • Switch off notifications and close other apps. A phone buzzing mid-recitation breaks concentration for a young child.
  • Know how to join and leave the call yourself so you are never dependent on the child.

Ask any academy about its approach to child safety: who can contact the family, through which channels, and whether lessons are ever recorded. Reputable academies answer these questions plainly.

Realistic expectations by age

  • Ages 4 to 6: short attention spans. Expect listening, letter sounds and games. Thirty-minute classes suit this age. Progress shows in confidence before it shows in reading.
  • Ages 7 to 10: the sweet spot for steady reading progress. Short daily practice makes a visible difference within weeks.
  • Ages 11 to 14: more able to take correction and set their own goals, but schedules compete with homework and activities. Involve them in choosing the lesson time.
  • Teens: respond to ownership. Let them choose the goal, whether that is fluency, Tajweed or memorisation.

If you are unsure whether your child is ready, this guide on the best age to start covers the signs to look for.

Homework without pressure

Good homework is small and specific, for example "read these four lines three times and mark the word you find hardest". It should take ten to fifteen minutes.

If homework regularly takes longer, tell the teacher. The target is too big, not the child too slow. Teachers adjust when they hear this early.

Talking to the teacher

Share what you notice: the child is tired on Tuesdays, gets anxious about reading aloud, or has started to enjoy a particular surah. Teachers plan better with that context.

Ask for a short progress summary every few weeks. If you want a method for this, see the progress tracking guide.

Warning signs worth acting on

  • Lessons are constantly cancelled or moved without notice.
  • The teacher does most of the reciting and the child barely speaks.
  • There is no clear plan or feedback after several weeks.
  • Your child dreads lessons for reasons that go beyond normal tiredness.

Any of these deserves a direct conversation with the academy. If nothing improves, trying a different teacher is reasonable; a free trial makes that low-risk.
