Tajweed Rules: 12 Essential Topics with Corrected Quran Examples

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

Tajweed helps a reader recite the Quran with accurate Arabic sounds and transmitted rules. It concerns articulation, letter qualities, vowel lengths and the ways words are joined or paused. An attractive voice is welcome, but clarity and correctness take priority over melody.

The twelve topics below follow the educational sequence of the supplied article. Several definitions and examples in that text needed correction, so those points are explained explicitly. Use this overview with a qualified teacher: written descriptions cannot fully demonstrate a sound.

What Tajweed means

The word is associated with making something good or improving it. In Quran recitation, that means giving letters their proper articulation and qualities, and observing the rules of the recognised reading being studied. Different transmitted readings and routes can have different details; begin with one clear teaching framework.

1. Noon sakinah and tanwin

Noon sakinah is ن without a short vowel. Tanwin is the doubled vowel marking that gives an ending n sound in connected recitation: an, in or un. The following letter determines the relevant rule. The pronunciation at a stop needs its own guidance.

Izhar: clear pronunciation

The six throat letters are ء، ه، ع، ح، غ، خ. Before these letters, noon sakinah or tanwin is pronounced clearly without converting or merging it. “Clear” does not mean exaggerating the n or inserting an extra vowel. An example is مِنْ هَادٍ, where noon is followed by ه.

Idgham: merging

In the usual beginner treatment for Hafs, the letters ي، ن، م، و produce idgham with ghunnah when the relevant noon or tanwin occurs at the end of one word and the next word begins with that letter. The letters ل and ر produce idgham without ghunnah. Learn the recognised exceptions and the route being followed rather than applying this automatically to every same-word occurrence.

The phrase مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ demonstrates a noon followed by ر in connected reading. A teacher can also model a joining example with ي or م. Ghunnah is a nasal resonance, not simply a sound from both the mouth and nose defined without attention to the nasal passage.

Iqlab: conversion

Before ب, noon sakinah or tanwin is converted in pronunciation to a concealed meem with ghunnah. The phrase مِنْ بَعْدِ is a common example. Learn the lip position through demonstration; do not pronounce an obvious separate n and m in succession.

Ikhfa: concealment

The fifteen letters are ت، ث، ج، د، ذ، ز، س، ش، ص، ض، ط، ظ، ف، ق، ك. The noon sound is concealed with ghunnah between clear pronunciation and full merging. In مِنْ شَرِّ, noon precedes ش. Avoid describing ikhfa as merely making a “blurry” sound; the articulation must prepare for the following letter.

2. Meem sakinah

Meem sakinah is م without a short vowel. Its three rules should be learned separately from noon sakinah, even though some labels resemble each other.

  • Ikhfa shafawi: before ب, conceal the meem with ghunnah, as in تَرْمِيهِمْ بِحِجَارَةٍ.
  • Idgham mithlayn saghir: when a non-vowelled meem meets a vowelled meem, merge into the second meem with ghunnah, as in لَهُمْ مَا.
  • Izhar shafawi: pronounce meem clearly before the other letters, taking care not to conceal it accidentally before similar lip sounds.

3. Madd: vowel elongation

The long-vowel letters are alif preceded by fathah, waw sakinah preceded by dammah, and ya sakinah preceded by kasrah. Merely seeing ا، و or ي does not make every occurrence a madd letter. The source's list using ى without explaining the vowel conditions was incomplete.

  • Natural madd: normally two counts, without a following cause such as hamzah or an original sukun. The long vowel in قَالَ illustrates the basic sound.
  • Madd arid li-s-sukun: when stopping produces a temporary sukun after a long vowel, the recognised options include two, four or six counts in the usual Hafs teaching.
  • Madd muttasil: a madd letter followed by hamzah in the same word, such as جَاءَ. The permitted count depends on the route; it is not universally limited to four.
  • Madd munfasil: the madd ends one word and hamzah begins the next. Again, learn the count applicable to the transmitted route, not a universal four-count rule.
  • Madd lazim: an original sukun after the madd, including certain doubled consonants, requires six counts in the standard teaching. The familiar example الضَّالِّينَ should be practised with a teacher.

Counts are relative to the recitation pace, not fixed seconds. Keep the proportions consistent and avoid combining options from unrelated routes without instruction.

4. Qalqalah

The five letters ق، ط، ب، ج، د are collected in قُطْبُ جَدٍ. When one carries sukun, it is released with the characteristic qalqalah sound. The effect is more apparent at a stop, particularly on a doubled letter, but it must not become an added fathah, kasrah or dammah.

The source described this as echoing the articulation. A more useful practical target is a controlled release without a new vowel. Compare a middle-of-word example such as يَجْعَلْ with stopping on أَحَدْ, and let a teacher assess the release.

5. Doubled noon and meem

A noon or meem with shaddah carries an emphasised ghunnah, commonly taught as two counts. Examples include إِنَّ and ثُمَّ. The relevant nasal sound remains when stopping on the doubled letter; do not erase it because the reading has reached a pause.

6. Istiadhah and basmalah

Istiadhah is seeking refuge in Allah from the accursed Satan: أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ. Quran 16:98 connects seeking refuge with reciting the Quran. Learn the wording and its place at the beginning of a reading session.

Basmalah is بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ. In the customary Hafs reading, begin a surah with it except Surah At-Tawbah. Starting within a surah needs guidance appropriate to the passage and recognised practice.

The source's claim that one istiadhah is sufficient for the entire Quran regardless of sessions is misleading. A continued reading and a new session are different situations. Likewise, basmalah within a surah should not be advertised as always improving “flow” regardless of the following meaning.

When joining the refuge formula, basmalah and the opening of a surah, the teacher explains the permitted stopping and joining combinations. Between two surahs in Hafs, three familiar options are stopping at each boundary, stopping after the previous surah then joining basmalah to the next, or joining all three. Do not join basmalah to the previous surah and then stop before the next, which could suggest it belongs to the preceding ending.

At the transition from Al-Anfal to At-Tawbah, no basmalah is used. A pause, a brief sakt or a continuous join are taught in the recognised framework. Starting At-Tawbah as a fresh session includes istiadhah without an opening basmalah. These details replace the source's oversimplified “either connect or leave it out” instruction.

7. Tafkhim and tarqiq: heavy and light sounds

Tafkhim is the heavier resonance of particular letters; tarqiq is lighter articulation. Neither is a command to shout, force the throat or add emphasis according to a word's importance. The heavy letters are خ، ص، ض، غ، ط، ق، ظ, with degrees according to their vowels and surroundings.

The source's examples include ط in تَقْطِيعٌ and ق in قَوْمٌ. The first word also begins with a different letter, ت, which should not be confused with ط. Light examples include س in سُرُورًا and ه in هُدًى. These are pronunciation illustrations, not claims that the particular forms all occur at the cited locations.

The alif follows the heaviness or lightness of the preceding letter. The special lam rule concerns the divine name Allah: its lam is heavy after fathah or dammah and light after kasrah. It does not apply to every lam according to the vowel that follows it, as the source suggested.

Ra has detailed rules involving its own vowel and, when non-vowelled, the surrounding vowels and letters. A vowelled ra with fathah or dammah is generally heavy, and with kasrah light in this framework. Learn the non-vowelled cases and exceptions rather than the source's vague claim of a moderate default.

8. Mudud: another look at elongation

The source called this topic “Muldood,” but the usual plural of madd is mudud. It is not a separate musical rule from the elongation already discussed. A madd sign in a mushaf helps indicate particular extended cases; not every natural long vowel carries that sign.

Quran 73:4 instructs measured recitation: وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا. The ya in تَرْتِيلًا has natural madd; the source's claim that a special maddah sign over that ya makes it a longer melodic elongation is inaccurate. Read the actual marking in the mushaf and apply the relevant rule.

9. Waqf and ibtida: stopping and restarting

Waqf concerns a pause in recitation, and ibtida concerns a start or restart. Both depend on the wording, grammar and meaning. Learn how the final letter is read when stopping, as well as whether the chosen point provides a complete or suitable sense.

The source uses the basmalah before Al-Fatihah as an example of beginning and pausing. That is a useful introductory illustration, but the numbering of Al-Fatihah's verses differs by counting tradition. “1:1–2” should not be used as if it resolves all conventions.

Follow the stop symbols in your particular mushaf and ask about their legend. If breath forces an unsuitable stop, learn where to restart so that the connected meaning is restored. Stopping correctly is more than choosing a comfortable place to breathe.

10. Ghunnah

Ghunnah is the nasal resonance associated with noon and meem, with different strengths and durations in different rules. It is not limited to a nasal letter followed by another nasal letter, nor is its purpose simply to improve a tune.

In كَعَصْفٍ مَأْكُولٍ from Quran 105:5, tanwin at the end of عَصْفٍ meets the following meem, producing idgham with ghunnah in connected recitation. The source incorrectly called this a meem sakinah followed by another meem. The visible shaddah in a teaching mushaf reflects the merging; it does not turn the first word's tanwin into an originally written meem.

11. Sifat al-huruf: letter qualities

Letter qualities distinguish sounds that may have nearby articulation points. A standard classification includes qualities with opposites and qualities without opposites. The source confused those categories with “continuous” and “discontinuous” breath and used an incorrect Arabic label.

Opposed qualities include hams and jahr, shiddah and rikhwah with an intermediate category, istiala and istifal, itbaq and infitah, and idhlaq and ismat. Qualities without direct opposites include such examples as qalqalah, safir, lin, inhiraf, takrir, tafashshi and istitālah in the familiar seventeen-quality classification. Teaching classifications can vary.

The source's ف، ح and ش are examples of letters with hams, where breath flows; this does not exhaust their other qualities. ع، ظ and غ should not be grouped as a single “discontinuous” category simply because the source describes vocal vibration. Classical Tajweed jahr is not identical to a modern phonetic voiced/unvoiced classification.

The supplied example الضَّرُورِيَّة begins with ض, not ظ; it cannot demonstrate ظ as claimed. Learn each letter's full set of qualities with actual examples rather than relying on an incorrectly labelled word.

12. Makharij al-huruf: articulation points

The familiar teaching of seventeen detailed points groups them into five regions. Some letters share an articulation point and are distinguished by their qualities, so it is inaccurate to say that every letter necessarily has a completely unique origin.

  • Al-jawf: the oral and throat cavity for the long vowels ا، و، ي when their conditions are present. Hamzah is not a jawf letter.
  • Al-halq: the throat, with ء and ه at its deepest part, ع and ح in the middle, and غ and خ at its upper part.
  • Al-lisan: the tongue, accounting for many letters. Qaf is articulated at the rear tongue against the relevant upper region, with kaf nearby; neither is a throat or jawf letter.
  • Ash-shafatān: the lips, including ب، م and non-madd و, with ف formed using the lower lip and upper teeth.
  • Al-khayshum: the nasal passage for ghunnah, not an alternative ordinary articulation point for all sounds of noon and meem.

The source's قلب example illustrates ق and ل, but Quran 2:7 has قُلُوبِهِمْ, not the singular word printed there. Lam uses the tongue's edge towards its tip against the upper gums. In Surah Al-Falaq, الْفَلَقِ occurs in 113:1, not 113:5; its qaf comes from the rear tongue, not al-jawf.

Other examples need similar care: أَبْ begins with hamzah articulated in the throat, not a long alif in the cavity; حَمْدٌ demonstrates ح; قَلْبٌ can illustrate qaf; and بِسْمِ demonstrates ba with the lips. The ordinary form مُنَافِقُونَ does not contain the noon shaddah inserted in the source, so it is not an example of a doubled noon.

Learning these rules through guided practice

Work from accurate letters to short passages, then to sound interactions and meaningful stops. A teacher should demonstrate a rule, hear your attempt and explain the correction. General advertising claims about the best provider, particular countries or guaranteed results cannot replace that assessment.

Continue with Quran pronunciation, Quran reading rules and learning Tajweed at home. Ask about an appropriate level through the course options and keep one recognised recitation route clear throughout study.
