How to Finish the Quran in Ramadan: Realistic Reading Plans

Talqeen Academy · Updated 4 October 2026, 23:32:31 (UTC+03:00)

Ramadan offers an important opportunity for Quran recitation and reflection. Quran 2:185 associates the month with the revelation of the Quran, while the night of its revelation is discussed in Surah Al-Qadr. A reading plan can help protect time for the text without turning completion into a race that sacrifices pronunciation or understanding.

To complete one reading, divide the whole Quran across the days available. Its thirty juz provide convenient divisions, but Ramadan may have twenty-nine days, and printed editions have different page counts. Calculate from the mushaf you actually use.

Why read the Quran during Ramadan?

Regular recitation can support worship, study and attention to guidance. Reflection on meaning may help connect fasting and prayer with character, relationships and responsibility. A completed reading is a valuable goal, but thoughtful engagement remains important throughout the month.

The source suggests reading more, improving understanding and aiming for a complete reading. These aims can work together when the schedule fits your current ability. Someone still learning the alphabet should not feel that slow corrected reading is worthless because it does not reach the same page total as a fluent reader.

Choose a completion goal and divide the text

The Quran is divided into thirty juz, making one juz a day a familiar thirty-day plan. These divisions do not necessarily end at a surah boundary. Following printed juz and hizb markers is more reliable than guessing equal portions from chapter lengths.

Use the number of days you can realistically read. If you want to finish within twenty-nine days, one juz on each of those days leaves one juz unread. Spread the extra portion across the month or reserve an appropriate additional session.

Audio recitation can model pronunciation; translation communicates meaning; Tafsir explains context and interpretation. Each helps differently. Listening to audio without reading is not automatically the same as your own recitation, and completing a translation is not the same activity as completing the Arabic text.

Etiquette and preparation

Choose a clean, suitable setting and approach the text respectfully. The source recommends wudu, clean clothes, istiadhah and basmalah. Distinguish etiquette from legal conditions: touching a physical mushaf, reciting from memory and using a device have separate scholarly discussions.

Learn the rules of purification from a teacher. Do not infer from a general reading schedule that every recitation without wudu is prohibited, or that all forms of reading have identical requirements. Begin with the refuge formula, and learn the place of basmalah, including the exception at the beginning of At-Tawbah.

Fajr, after Asr, before study or work, and a quiet evening period are examples of possible reading times. Choose a time that supports attentive reading. Ramadan does not automatically produce the same energy or emotional state for every person.

Make the intention sincere and the plan practical

Ask Allah for sincerity, guidance and beneficial understanding. The source suggests that a sincere intention always brings enough time, health and energy to complete the goal. That is not a guaranteed outcome: illness, caring duties and other constraints may remain even for a sincere learner.

Determination helps a person return after interruptions, but it should not become pressure to ignore genuine limits. If a verse is difficult, slow down, obtain correction and study its meaning. Do not rush past an error simply to remain on a page target.

Keep a stable routine where possible, yet adjust it when circumstances require. Another person's two-juz goal need not become your own. Quran 49:13, quoted in the source, concerns peoples, tribes and knowing one another; it is not a direct instruction about individual reading schedules or learning styles.

The twenty-page plan: useful with a qualification

For an edition containing exactly 600 reading pages, 600 ÷ 30 = 20 pages a day. Across five daily prayers, 20 ÷ 5 = 4 pages per prayer period. The source mistakenly says to subtract five from twenty to obtain four; the correct operation is division.

A commonly used Madinah mushaf has 604 pages, so twenty pages daily for thirty days leaves four pages. Page counts and layout vary. Use the actual count, or follow one complete juz per day plus an adjustment for a twenty-nine-day month.

For 600 pages over twenty-nine days, the average is approximately 20.69 pages daily. For 604 pages, it is approximately 20.83. In practice, allocate small additional portions or use the printed divisions rather than stopping mid-sentence to match a decimal page target.

Four ways to distribute a daily portion

  • After each prayer: four pages at each of five prayer periods gives twenty pages.
  • Before and after prayer: two pages before and two after each period gives the same total.
  • Four reading periods: five pages in the morning, around midday, in the evening and at night gives twenty pages.
  • Two reading periods: ten pages in the morning and ten in the evening gives twenty pages.

These are distribution examples for the 600-page, thirty-day model. Add the remaining pages required by your edition and available days. Check that the allocated times allow careful reading rather than assuming four pages take the same time for every learner.

If you miss one four-page session, add those four pages at another suitable time. The source says to read four pages before and after the next prayer, which makes eight at that period and can recover the missed four. The important point is the day's correct total, not a rigid placement around worship.

Using juz divisions instead of pages

One juz daily completes thirty juz in thirty days. You can divide a juz into its printed subdivisions or suitable passages across the day. A juz is often about twenty pages in a particular layout, but that approximation is not universal.

The source's example of the first juz contains several numerical errors. It does not contain all 286 verses of Al-Baqarah. In the familiar division it includes Al-Fatihah and Al-Baqarah 1–141. Al-Fatihah has seven verses in the usual count, not 141 remaining verses.

The statement about “286 verses from Al-Baqarah and Al-Fatihah,” the later number 141 and the proposed split of 28 into 14 with an instruction to read four are therefore not a coherent schedule. If you divide Al-Baqarah 1–141 over five periods, about 28–29 verses is a numerical average, but verse lengths differ substantially.

Prefer meaningful passage boundaries and printed subdivisions. Equal verse counts do not guarantee equal reading time, and memorizing Al-Fatihah does not remove it from a plan to recite the whole Quran.

One, two or three completions

For a thirty-day month, one completion requires thirty juz in total, two require sixty, and three require ninety. The corresponding daily averages are one, two and three juz. For twenty-nine days, the averages become approximately 1.034, 2.069 and 3.103 juz respectively.

In the simplified 600-page model, two completions mean forty pages daily across thirty days; eight pages after each of five prayers produces that total. Three completions mean sixty pages daily, or twelve pages at each of those five periods. Adjust again for the actual edition and days available.

More completions are not automatically the right goal. Keep accurate recitation, understanding and your other responsibilities in view. A beginner's progress may be best measured by correct reading of a smaller portion.

Overcoming fear of not finishing

Begin with a manageable portion rather than postponing until you can guarantee success. Keep a simple record of the last passage read, and return after a missed session without abandoning the entire plan.

The source suggests that some people may take decades to reach a Ramadan completion. There is no need to make that an expected timeline or a threat. Pace varies with fluency, available time and circumstances. A partial reading can still be meaningful worship and a foundation for continued practice.

Adjusting the goal is different from careless reading. If the chosen target encourages guessing, breathless speed or neglect of essential duties, reduce it and strengthen the quality of the reading.

Family and group reading

A family or study group can provide encouragement, discussion and accountability. The source gives an example of five household members reading six juz each, so the group collectively covers thirty juz. That does not mean each person individually recited the whole Quran.

Be clear about the activity being measured: a shared distribution, listening to others and a personal complete recitation are distinct. Do not guarantee that a distributed reading confers the identical reward of individual completion on every participant without evidence.

A partner can help maintain a routine and discuss meaning, while a qualified teacher checks pronunciation. Choose a supportive arrangement that helps you engage with the text rather than adding embarrassment or competition.

A daily habit that continues after Ramadan

  1. Choose an anchor such as after Fajr, before iftar or before sleep.
  2. Set a manageable amount in pages, printed subdivisions or a suitable passage.
  3. Read with attention to pronunciation and use translation or Tafsir for meaning.
  4. Consider one practical lesson for your conduct.
  5. Record the stopping point and return consistently.
  6. Invite a suitable partner when shared study is helpful.

Devotional reading can follow the Quran in sequence or focus on a chosen passage for study. Do not read an isolated verse as a prediction or a private instruction detached from context. Reflection should be connected with reliable explanation.

Reading English meanings alongside Arabic

English translations can help someone who does not yet understand Arabic engage with the Quran's message. Read the translator's introduction, check context and consult reliable Tafsir when a passage is unclear. Translation remains an interpretation of meaning, not the original Arabic text.

A daily English reading can support reflection, while separate Arabic practice develops recitation. See reading the Quran in English and understanding its meaning for a clearer distinction.

Benefits, listening and responsible expectations

The source cites the report about fasting and the Quran interceding for a person, found in Mishkat al-Masabih 1963. Read the report with its source and assessment rather than attach invented reward amounts to a completion plan.

Recitation can support spiritual growth, attention to duties, discipline, gratitude and comfort. The source also promises physical healing and overcoming depression and anxiety. Those are not guaranteed clinical effects of finishing the Quran; spiritual comfort can accompany, rather than replace, appropriate care.

Listening attentively is valuable, but it is not the same action as personally reciting with the tongue. Avoid a blanket claim that one always receives greater reward solely because more senses are involved. If your goal is your own Arabic completion, track the text you actually recite and label listening separately.

Is completing the Quran in Ramadan obligatory?

A full completion is not a general obligation imposed on every Muslim during Ramadan. This does not remove the Quran recitation required within prayer or other particular duties. Pursue a realistic goal without calling someone sinful merely because they did not finish the entire text.

Reading the whole Quran in one day may be physically possible for some highly fluent readers, but speed can undermine understanding and accuracy. The prophetic guidance concerning very rapid readings deserves attention; it is not sensible to market a one-day completion as the default plan for beginners.

Use reading guidance, pronunciation support and a sustainable routine to make the month productive. The best personal schedule is one that supports accurate worship and meaningful engagement within your real circumstances.
