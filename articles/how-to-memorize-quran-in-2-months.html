Can You Memorize the Quran in 2 Months? A Realistic 60-Day Plan

Talqeen Academy · Updated 5 October 2026, 08:35:26 (UTC+03:00)

Memorising the entire Quran in two months is an exceptionally demanding target. The source proposed a sixty-day schedule for someone with strong memory and previous experience. It also described the methods as proven and suggested that many learners finish even sooner, without supplying evidence for those general claims.

A useful plan begins with an assessment of reading accuracy, existing memorisation and available time. For most beginners, a longer timetable is more appropriate. A sixty-day target should remain adjustable, with correction and revision taking priority over reaching a date. First-time memorisation is also different from durable retention of the complete Quran.

Calculate the Actual Daily Workload

Using the commonly printed Kufan total of 6,236 ayahs, division by sixty gives approximately 103.93 ayahs each day. Rounding to 104 creates a convenient planning average, but verses vary greatly in length. One hundred short verses and one hundred long verses do not represent equal workloads.

The source used approximately 600 pages. In a 604-page edition, the average is about 10.07 pages daily; using 600 pages gives ten. Neither calculation applies to every printed layout. Plan against the actual mushaf you use and meaningful passage boundaries, rather than transferring a page target between editions.

The proposed rate of twenty ayahs an hour would require about 5.2 hours for 104 ayahs. This explains the source's estimate of five to six hours, but the rate is an assumption, not a measured result for every learner. It also leaves out the additional time required for correction, old revision and full recitation tests.

1. Allocate Time after Testing Your Learning Rate

Before committing to the daily target, complete several supervised practice sessions and record how long accurate recall actually takes. Include the time spent checking errors. If the pace requires more hours than your responsibilities allow, extend the deadline or reduce the new portion.

The original five-to-six-hour suggestion can describe the new-learning block for a suitably prepared student under its assumptions. It should not become a reason to feel guilty about a slower pace. A learner who completes fewer verses accurately and retains them is making meaningful progress.

2. Use Tahajjud or Fajr Time When It Suits Your Routine

The source recommended study around Tahajjud and Fajr. Quiet periods may suit some learners, and the virtue of night prayer is addressed in Sahih Muslim 1163a. Quran 17:78 mentions the dawn recitation being witnessed.

These references do not promise a specific memorisation rate or establish a scientific advantage for every learner. Choose a time when you can attend carefully and maintain adequate rest. A consistent daytime session can be more workable than an early session sustained through exhaustion.

3. Apply the Source's Three-Line, Ten-Repetition Exercise

In this article, the “3.10” method means learning three lines and repeating them ten times. Other guides may use a similar label differently, so establish the definition rather than assuming it is a standard technical term. Read the selected lines accurately, work on recall and connect them with the preceding passage.

Ten repetitions are an exercise count, not a guarantee against forgetting. A passage may need fewer or more attempts. After repetition, close the mushaf and test recall, then return to any error. Revisit the same material later and ask a teacher to check what repeated practice might have reinforced incorrectly.

4. Recite Aloud at a Comfortable Volume

Audible recitation lets you attend to what you are saying and provides material a teacher can correct. Keep the voice clear and comfortable; loudness itself does not prove concentration or accuracy. Listen for omitted words, uncertain joins and pronunciation that differs from the model.

The source invoked the instruction to beautify recitation with the voice. The relevant report is Abu Dawud 1468. Its devotional guidance should not be treated as evidence that raising the voice produces a guaranteed sixty-day result. Independent feedback remains necessary because learners can overlook their own repeated errors.

5. Divide Each Page into Manageable Sections

The source suggested three or four sections of roughly four to five lines on a sixteen-line page. Preserve this as an example of chunking, rather than a property of every mushaf. Many familiar editions use fifteen lines, and other layouts differ. Four groups of four lines would cover a sixteen-line page, while other combinations need adjustment.

Prefer boundaries that respect the verse and meaning where possible. Learn one section, test it and join it to the next. After completing the page, recite across all its joins and into the following page. Memorising isolated pieces without checking their transitions can leave gaps during a continuous recitation.

6. Use Audio as a Supporting Practice

Listening during a break or another suitable period can familiarise you with the passage's sound. Use a clear recitation appropriate to your learning needs and follow the wording when practical. Ask about a confusing sound instead of copying an unfamiliar delivery without understanding it.

The source suggested that the brain could absorb material without effort. Passive exposure should not be described as a substitute for deliberate recall. To evaluate learning, recite from memory and check against the mushaf or a competent listener. Audio supports this process; it does not establish that the passage has been memorised.

7. Protect Focus without Ignoring Other Responsibilities

Choose a workable study space and reduce avoidable interruptions from notifications, background noise and unrelated conversation. Tell family members when a focused session is planned so the arrangement can be realistic. Keep the mushaf and the materials needed for correction within reach.

A focused schedule still needs breaks and room for essential commitments. If attention falls sharply, review a familiar portion or pause before continuing. The purpose is accurate engagement, not remaining at the desk for an impressive number of hours while errors accumulate.

8. Arrange Teacher Supervision and Daily Review

The original article recommended a tutor for four practical reasons: correcting mistakes, selecting methods for the learner, maintaining accountability through daily review, and offering encouragement. Confirm how those services are actually provided, including when new and older portions are tested.

  • Check pronunciation and wording before consolidating the new passage.
  • Adapt the portion and method to observed performance.
  • Set review appointments and track recurring mistakes.
  • Use feedback to make the next session achievable.

A competent teacher cannot guarantee that every learner will finish in sixty days. If older passages weaken, reduce the new load and rebuild recall. Read the revision guide and memorisation timeline comparisons before selecting a deadline.

Define Completion and Plan Continued Revision

Ask what completion means: encountering every passage, reciting each newly learned portion once, or retaining the whole Quran for independent testing. A short intensive programme may reach an initial milestone while still needing substantial consolidation afterwards. Keep a continuing schedule for recent and older material.
